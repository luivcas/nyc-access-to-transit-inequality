1 Step 1 — Observation

Where Is Public Transport
Concentrated?

New York City operates one of the world's largest subway networks — 472 stations spanning 245 miles of track. Yet the distribution of stations is far from uniform. The map below shows subway lines, stations, and their walking-distance buffers.

Rush hour at 42nd Street — the daily reality of millions of New York City commuters
  • Manhattan is saturated with stations — almost every block is within easy walking distance of a stop.
  • Large coverage gaps appear in eastern Queens, southeastern Brooklyn, and nearly all of Staten Island.
  • The outer boroughs depend heavily on slower, less frequent bus networks to fill these gaps.

"But access only matters when people actually need it. To understand inequality, we must first ask: where do people live?"

A Step 2 — Analysis

Where Do People
Need Public Transport?

Population density maps reveal where transit demand is highest. When overlaid with subway infrastructure, a structural mismatch begins to appear — dense communities are not always the ones with the strongest coverage.

Commuters waiting at 168th Street station — Washington Heights, The Bronx
  • Significant density exists well outside Manhattan — in the Bronx, central Brooklyn, and western Queens.
  • These densely populated zones often coincide with the transit gaps identified in the previous map.
  • This mismatch is not accidental — it reflects decades of unequal infrastructure investment.

Key finding: High population density does not guarantee strong transit access. This mismatch begins to reveal structural inequality.

4 Step 3 — Comparison

Who Has Access —
and Who Does Not?

When socioeconomic data is layered onto transit access, a clear pattern emerges. Lower-income communities are disproportionately concentrated in zones with the weakest subway coverage — compounding existing disadvantage.

Without Transit Access — Population Below Federal Poverty Line
Affordable Housing Units — Extremely Low Income
  • Lower-income neighborhoods frequently lack proximity to frequent, reliable subway service.
  • Communities below the federal poverty line show the longest average walk times to the nearest station.
  • Affordable housing is often concentrated in areas with weaker transit coverage.

Key insight: Transit inequality is both spatial and social. Where you live shapes what you can access — and what opportunities are within reach.

N Step 5 — Case Studies

Three Boroughs,
Three Realities

Comparing three neighborhoods illustrates how geography, income, and infrastructure converge to create radically different transit experiences within the same city.

Manhattan — High Access
High Access

Manhattan

  • One of the world's densest subway networks — a station within 5 min walk almost anywhere
  • High accessibility across income levels; 51% of workers commute by public transit
  • Efficient cross-borough connections via multiple intersecting lines
Eastern Queens — Low Access
Low Access

Eastern Queens

  • Few stations — many residents travel 20–40 min just to reach a subway line
  • Heavy reliance on buses that are slower, less frequent, and more crowded
  • Only 46% of Queens workers commute by public transit; 39% drive
South Bronx — High Need
High Need

South Bronx

  • Transit exists but is strained — high demand from a dense, lower-income population
  • Highest transit dependency in the city (56%) with few car-ownership alternatives
  • Overcrowded trains and limited service frequency compound everyday hardship
7 Conclusion

Unequal Access,
Unequal Opportunity

Access to public transportation is not distributed equally across New York City. The subway system — though vast — is concentrated in areas that were already well-served, leaving large populations in the outer boroughs without reliable, frequent service.

Geography and income are the two strongest predictors of transit access. Those who live furthest from subway stations are, on average, those who can least afford the time or financial cost of longer, fragmented commutes.

Inequality in transit does not exist in isolation — it amplifies inequality in employment, education, healthcare, and social mobility. Closing the transit gap is not merely a transportation question. It is a question of social justice.

The city that never sleeps runs on a transit system that does not serve all its residents equally. Addressing this requires not just new lines, but a fundamental rethinking of who infrastructure is built for — and who gets left behind.
